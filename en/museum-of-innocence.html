Happiness After the Fact: Mourning, Collecting and Desire in The Museum of Innocence

Haktan Tay · 19 October 2026 · 22 min read

Orhan Pamuk’s The Museum of Innocence is a novel that ends in a museum, and a museum that begins with a novel. This essay reads that double structure through three old questions of psychoanalysis. What does it mean that a happiness can only be known as happiness once it has been lost? What forms can a life with a lost object take? And what kind of relation to a loss becomes possible when a man sets it out, piece by piece, before the eyes of strangers?

This essay first appeared in Turkish as “Sonradan Gelen Mutluluk”. The English version is my own; it follows the Turkish closely, but expands the passages that depend on Turkish grammar for readers who do not know the language.

“It was the happiest moment of my life, though I didn’t know it.”

Orhan Pamuk, The Museum of Innocence, trans. Maureen Freely

Jede Leidenschaft grenzt ja ans Chaos, die sammlerische aber an das der Erinnerungen.

Walter Benjamin, “Ich packe meine Bibliothek aus”

I

The first sentence of the novel is the key not only to the book but to the museum that was built alongside it. In Maureen Freely’s English it reads: “It was the happiest moment of my life, though I didn’t know it.” On a first reading we hear it as an expression of sorrow, a retrospective regret, a lament for a happiness that slipped past unnoticed. But the weight of the sentence lies less in what it says than in its grammar, and the grammar is precisely what an English sentence cannot carry. Kemal Basmacı is not telling us that he was happy. He is telling us that he had been happy, that he lived this happiness without knowing it, and that he is only now finding it out. When this “now” takes place, the novel does not say. Only much later do we understand that we have been listening to the voice of a man who has left the whole catastrophe of his story behind him and arranged what he lived in the display cases of a museum. The first sentence speaks from the end of the novel; it is a closing word placed at the very beginning of the narrative.1

The Turkish original has five words: Hayatımın en mutlu anıymış, bilmiyordum. Everything turns on one suffix, the -mış attached to an, “moment”. Turkish grammars traditionally call the past tense built on this suffix the “learned” or “reported” past (it changes its vowel to -miş, -muş or -müş by vowel harmony). It tells the listener that the speaker did not witness the event but knows of it indirectly, by inference or by hearsay. Its counterpart, -dı, marks the past the speaker has seen and vouches for. Linguists have drawn attention to a further, subtler use of -mış: it can register the surprise of a speaker who meets a piece of information for which the mind was not prepared. The pioneering study of the Turkish evidential published by Dan Slobin and Ayhan Aksu in 1982, and the concept of mirativity that Scott DeLancey introduced into linguistic typology in 1997, drawing largely on that study, showed that this suffix is the grammatical trace of exactly such a moment of astonishment.2 I cannot pass over a small coincidence here that makes one’s head spin. In the situation Slobin and Aksu chose to illustrate this use, the speaker opens the door, finds an entirely unexpected visitor, and exclaims: Kemal gelmiş! “Why, Kemal has come!”3 Pamuk’s Kemal enters the novel in just this way: as a man who opens the door of his own life and finds there a guest he never expected, a happiness that has arrived too late.

Every translation of the sentence must decide where to put the surprise that Turkish packs into a single syllable. Freely moves it into the second clause, “though I didn’t know it”, and leaves the first in a plain past, “It was”. Valérie Gay-Aksoy’s French makes the same choice: « C’était le moment le plus heureux de ma vie, je ne le savais pas. » Neither choice is a fault; English and French have no grammatical category that does the work of -mış, and both translators rightly prefer a clean sentence to an explanatory one. But the reader without Turkish should know what has been lost on the way, because the rest of this essay depends on it. In Turkish, the suffix itself says that the happiness was not witnessed by the man who lived it. He finds it out as one finds out a piece of news: after the fact, from somewhere else, and with a jolt.

Psychoanalysis had come across this structure long before linguistics found it in -mış, and under another name. In 1895, before psychoanalysis even had a name, Freud wrote a manuscript he never published, the Project for a Scientific Psychology. Analysing the fear of a young woman he calls Emma, who could not go into shops alone, he encounters a strange temporality. An assault that took place in a shop when she was eight is not traumatic when it happens. It acquires its traumatic value only retroactively, when a second scene in puberty, insignificant in itself, gives the first a new meaning.4 Freud would call this backward-working causality Nachträglichkeit. The concept reaches its most mature form in the Wolf Man case, published in 1918, where a long analysis shows that the meaning of a scene witnessed by a child of one and a half was constituted only by a dream at the age of four.5 That the concept moved to the centre of psychoanalytic thought, under its French name après-coup, was the work first of Lacan and then of Laplanche and Pontalis. James Strachey’s rendering in the Standard Edition, “deferred action”, had largely blurred its double direction: a delay, and at the same time an effect that runs backwards. It was to recover that second direction that Laplanche later proposed a new English word for it, afterwardsness.6

The sentence in which Lacan formulates this temporality in his Rome discourse of 1953 stands in something like a mirror relation to the first sentence of The Museum of Innocence. Speaking in the first person, Lacan says that what is realised in my history cannot be the definite past of what was, since it is no more, nor even the perfect of what has been in what I am; it is the future anterior of what I will have been for what I am in the process of becoming.7 Analytic time is thus a past that opens towards the future: the subject rewrites his past according to what he will turn out to have been. Kemal’s -mış is like a closed version of the same structure. He too rewrites the past from the present; but his rewriting arrives not at a future but at a museum. Where the future anterior of analysis makes movement possible for the subject, Kemal’s mirative past freezes that movement behind the glass of a display case. The real drama of the novel, as I read it, is knotted in the tension between these two tenses, in the question of whether afterwardsness will turn out to be a liberation or a prison. When we reach the novel’s final sentence we shall see that the question receives an unexpected grammatical answer.

II

The outline of the story is well known. In the spring of 1975, Kemal, the thirty-year-old son of a wealthy textile family from Nişantaşı, an affluent and self-consciously Westernised district of Istanbul, is about to become engaged to Sibel, a woman who is in every respect a “suitable” match: by education, by family, by circle. In the boutique where he goes to buy Sibel a handbag he meets Füsun, a distant and poor relation of his family, who is eighteen. Their meetings continue in a flat in the Merhamet Apartments, where Kemal’s family keeps its old furniture. Merhamet means “mercy”, and the novel never once remarks on the irony of the name; it simply lets it go on ringing in the reader’s ear for hundreds of pages.

Kemal’s position in this first period is almost a textbook example of the structure Freud described in 1912 as the most prevalent form of degradation in erotic life. Speaking of men who cannot unite the current of tenderness with the current of sensuality, Freud writes a sentence that would be quoted countless times: Wo sie lieben, begehren sie nicht, und wo sie begehren, können sie nicht lieben; where they love they do not desire, and where they desire they cannot love.8 Sibel, the approved woman, the one to marry, is placed at the pole of tenderness; Füsun, positioned “below” by class and social standing, at the pole of desire. What separates Pamuk’s novel from an ordinary love triangle is that Kemal’s belief that he is entitled to maintain this split, his fantasy of giving up neither woman, is patiently and mercilessly dismantled in the novel’s opening chapters.

The social frame of this collapse should not be overlooked. The Istanbul bourgeoisie of the 1970s hangs between the sexual freedom promised by Westernisation and an older moral order in which virginity still determines a woman’s social value. With a quiet but unmistakable clarity, the novel shows that Sibel and Füsun do not pay the same price for their relationship with the same man. Deniz Kandiyoti’s notion of “bargaining with patriarchy” is apt here. Within the limits of the patriarchal order in which they live, women develop different strategies to protect or improve their position, and which of these strategies is available to a woman is not independent of her class.9 Sibel’s bargain and Füsun’s are not struck at the same table.

The fantasy collapses on the night of the engagement party. Füsun comes, sees Kemal at Sibel’s side, and from the next day on she disappears. Every day Kemal goes to the Merhamet Apartments at the same hour and waits. Here begins the most psychoanalytically dense section of the novel, for desire gives away its structure most clearly at the point where its object withdraws. While Füsun is there, there is a relationship. When Füsun is gone, what remains is desire alone: without an object, without an address, and for that very reason no longer to be appeased by anything.

III

In Füsun’s absence Kemal begins to live with the objects she touched in the flat. He picks up a hair slide, a pen, a tea glass, presses it to his cheek, to his neck, and feels his pain ease for a moment. The novel recounts these scenes without any recourse to irony, with the precision of a medical textbook and the repetitiveness of a prayer book. The first concept to come to mind is surely fetishism, and the first impression is not entirely wrong. In his short, dense paper of 1927, Freud defines the fetish as the product of a refusal: the subject perceives a lack and yet refuses to accept the verdict of that perception, a refusal Freud calls Verleugnung, disavowal. The fetish is the monument to this refusal, an object that makes it possible both to know and not to know the lack at the same time.10 Some forty years later, in an essay that extends this logic into a whole theory of belief, Octave Mannoni fits it into one of the plainest sentences in French: Je sais bien, mais quand même… I know very well, but all the same…11 Kemal knows that Füsun has gone; but her hair slide is here, all the same.

Yet to exhaust Kemal’s relation to objects with the concept of fetishism would be to miss the novel’s main vein. “Mourning and Melancholia”, written by Freud in 1915 and published in 1917, offers a much finer distinction. For Freud, mourning is the libido’s submission to the verdict of reality-testing that the loved object no longer exists; but the submission does not happen all at once. Each memory, each expectation attached to the lost person is called up, in Freud’s words, “bit by bit” (Stück für Stück), hypercathected, and only then detached.12 Mourning in this sense is a farewell carried out by touching the loss piece by piece, and what Kemal does is the exact reverse of this work. He too touches his memories one by one; but instead of detaching each memory he touches, he binds it to an object, keeps it, and moves on to the next. The farewell that mourning carries out piece by piece becomes, in his hands, a preservation carried out piece by piece.

Freud’s formula for distinguishing melancholia from mourning comes to mind of its own accord: Bei der Trauer ist die Welt arm und leer geworden, bei der Melancholie ist es das Ich selbst; in mourning it is the world that has become poor and empty, in melancholia it is the ego itself. The melancholic cannot give up the lost object; he identifies with it, and, in Freud’s unforgettable image, the shadow of the object falls upon the ego.13 Kemal’s existence in the middle sections of the novel is lived inside this shadow. He withdraws more and more from his work, his family, the social life of his class; his life becomes an orbit narrowing around what remains of Füsun.

The real question about Kemal’s loss, however, is asked by Lacan, who deliberately turns Freud’s framework inside out. In his tenth seminar, on anxiety, given in 1962–1963, Lacan finds Freud’s theory of mourning insufficient and offers this provocative sentence: Nous ne sommes en deuil que de quelqu’un dont nous pouvons nous dire : j’étais son manque. We mourn only someone of whom we can say to ourselves: I was their lack.14 Mourning, on this reading, concerns not the gap the other leaves in us but the place we occupied in the other’s desire; it is the loss of our position as what the other lacked. To reread The Museum of Innocence in the light of this sentence is unsettling, for the novel never settles whether Kemal was ever what Füsun lacked. What Kemal mourns is perhaps not Füsun but the short time in which he could believe that he was a lack for her: the afternoons in the Merhamet Apartments, the innocence the novel takes as its title. Seen this way, the name of the museum reads almost like a diagnosis. Innocence, here, is the name of the time in which one’s place in the other’s desire has not yet been doubted.

IV

To understand Kemal’s collection we must step outside psychoanalysis for a moment and turn to a short talk Walter Benjamin gave in the summer of 1931. Benjamin had moved his library to a new flat and was still unpacking the crates when, in what may be the most beautiful text ever written on collecting, he wrote the sentence I have taken as an epigraph. In Harry Zohn’s translation: “Every passion borders on the chaotic, but the collector’s passion borders on the chaos of memories.” For the true collector, Benjamin says, ownership is the most intimate relationship one can have to objects; but its intimacy does not come from the objects coming to life in the collector. It is the other way round: nicht daß sie in ihm lebendig wären, er selber ist es, der in ihnen wohnt, “not that they come alive in him; it is he who lives in them.” Having built a dwelling of books before his listeners, Benjamin ends the talk with a sentence in which the collector disappears inside it, as is only fitting.15

These sentences anticipate, almost like a prophecy, the way Kemal spends the last years of his life in the attic of his own museum, at the top of the old house of Füsun’s family, with his bed and his belongings. Visitors to the museum in Çukurcuma today find on its top floor a narrow iron bed, an old suitcase and a child’s tricycle that readers of the novel will recognise at once (Figure 2). The collector, as Benjamin said, has disappeared inside his collection. In his The System of Objects of 1968, Jean Baudrillard gives one subsection of his chapter on collecting a title that sums up the logic of this disappearance on its own: Un discours à soi-même, a discourse addressed to oneself.16 A collection is the form in which a subject tells himself the story of his own loss, over and over again.

This view also says something about the history of psychoanalysis itself. Over his lifetime Freud gathered a collection of some two thousand antiquities, filled his consulting room with Egyptian, Greek and Roman figurines, and listened to his patients under the gaze of these small objects. His famous conversation with the Rat Man, in which he compares the unconscious to Pompeii, takes place in the shadow of this collection: what lies buried has been preserved because it was buried; the moment it is brought to light, its destruction begins.17 Kemal’s museum reverses the image. What will preserve his objects is not the darkness of the soil but the glass of the vitrine; and the coming to light that in Pompeii marks the beginning of decay becomes, in Çukurcuma, a kind of sealing.

Is there anything that separates this collection from the fetish and from melancholia? The most apt answer comes, unexpectedly, from an anecdote Lacan tells in his seminar on ethics. Discussing sublimation in the winter of 1960, Lacan recalls a collection he had seen at the home of the poet Jacques Prévert: empty matchboxes, slid into one another so as to form a ribbon running along the mantelpiece and the wall.18 What struck Lacan was that the individual boxes had no value at all, and that the whole effect of the collection came from their arrangement in repetition, from the void that this arrangement brought into view. The most quoted formula of sublimation appears in the same seminar: sublimation raises an object to the dignity of the Thing, that is, to the place of the absolute privation that can never be possessed and can only be circled.19

The wall of cigarette stubs that greets visitors at the entrance of the museum is an almost literal counterpart of what Lacan saw on Prévert’s mantelpiece (Figure 3). Four thousand two hundred and thirteen stubs, each one by itself a nothing, the worthless residue of an act of consumption; yet set out side by side, row upon row, they make visible an absence that none of them could carry alone. The stub is an almost didactic example of what Lacan called the object-cause of desire, the objet petit a: not the person as a whole but a piece detached from her; the last trace of a pair of lips, the remainder left behind by a look, a voice, a gesture of the hand.20 Where Freud thinks of the fetish as a veil, Lacan’s sublimation builds a frame; and Kemal’s story is, I would argue, the story of a long and painful passage between these two operations. The hair slide he pressed to his cheek in the Merhamet Apartments was a fetish trying to cover an absence. The 4,213 stubs pinned to the museum wall are a structure that draws the outline of that absence.

V

When Füsun reappears a year later she is married. Her husband, Feridun, is a young man who wants to make films; so that the film in which Füsun is to star can be made, Kemal founds a company, Lemon Films, and puts up the capital himself. For the next eight years Kemal joins the family for dinner in the house in Çukurcuma, at a table shared by Füsun’s mother and father and by her husband. The television is on, rakı is poured, and a love that is never mentioned is restaged every evening with the same ceremonial care.

These eight years recall the thesis of the famous book in which Denis de Rougemont, in 1939, analysed the structure of Western love through the legend of Tristan: passion feeds on obstacles; the lovers love not so much each other as love itself, and the obstacle that intensifies it.21 The lessons Lacan devotes to medieval courtly love in his seminar on ethics rebuild the same structure in psychoanalytic language. The Lady is exalted in the measure that she is inaccessible, and the formula of sublimation I mentioned above finds its purest instance here.22 Kemal’s dinner table is a scene of courtly love set in a neighbourhood house in twentieth-century Istanbul. The most striking detail of the scene is that Kemal finances Feridun’s company. A lover who supplies his rival with capital is investing, in both the figurative and the literal sense of the word, in the continuation of the obstacle.

When the obstacles fall away, catastrophe comes. Füsun’s father dies and her marriage ends; she makes it a condition that Kemal introduce her to his family and that there be a formal engagement and a wedding. Kemal, Füsun, her mother Nesibe and the family’s driver Çetin set off by car for Paris. On the morning after a night spent in a hotel in Babaeski, the union awaited for nine years, Füsun is at the wheel; the car crashes, Füsun is killed, Kemal is badly injured. The novel leaves open whether the crash was an accident or something else, and the reader is left alone with the question. A psychoanalytic reading should not try to close it. But it can underline a structure: a desire nourished for nine years by an obstacle hits a wall after the first night on which the obstacle is gone. Lacan’s late proposition il n’y a pas de rapport sexuel, “there is no sexual relationship”, cannot of course mean anything as crude as that people do not make love. What it says is that the fantasy of two halves joining to make a whole cannot be written.23 Even a union awaited for nine years cannot become a completion; in every encounter something is missed, and in the novel it is Füsun who pays for this miss with her life.

At this point the question that the novel, or rather Kemal’s narrative, keeps hidden comes into view: what did Füsun want? Füsun wants to be an actress; but throughout the novel this desire circulates through other people’s money, other people’s approval, other people’s eyes. It is Kemal who tells us about Füsun, and Füsun’s voice is heard from inside the collection of the man who loves her. The museum is, from this angle, a structure that turns a woman’s life into the catalogue of a man’s desire. The greatness of the novel lies in carrying out this transformation and, at the same time, allowing the reader to notice it. To be the object of desire can mean being unable to become the subject of one’s own; The Museum of Innocence is a novel that carries the full weight of that sentence.

VI

Once he has recovered, Kemal decides to turn the Keskins’ house in Çukurcuma into a museum; but he does not write his story himself. He goes to Orhan Pamuk and asks him to write the novel that will serve as the museum’s catalogue. In the last chapter the narrator changes: Pamuk narrates Kemal’s death, and at Kemal’s request the book closes with the sentence “Let everyone know, I lived a very happy life.” Today the sentence is also written, in Turkish, on a wall of the museum (Figure 4).

“A Modest Manifesto for Museums”, which Pamuk wrote after opening the museum, offers a useful background for understanding the public meaning of this last sentence. The manifesto argues that the great national museums present the history of a nation as if it mattered more than the stories of individuals; it likens the transformation of museums from palaces into national institutions to the passage from epic to novel; and it ends by declaring that the future of museums lies inside people’s own homes.24 The Museum of Innocence is the first application of this programme: against the history of a nation, the museum of an individual, and of a passion that in the eyes of his world had turned into a disaster.

Let everyone know. To whom is this sentence addressed? What Lacan calls the big Other, the witness by whom the subject hopes to have his truth recognised, takes on here the body of the museum visitor and the reader. Kemal’s happiness will become real only when a witness confirms it, and the museum is the place of that witnessing. In one of his late seminars Lacan reads James Joyce’s writing as an invention that holds together a subjective world on the verge of coming apart and wins for it a name; he calls this invention the sinthome.25 The sinthome is less the name of a cure than the name of a singular solution that allows a subject to live with his symptom and to make a work out of it. Kemal’s museum can be read as such a solution. It does not bring back what was lost, but it allows a life and a name to be built around the loss. With one difference: Joyce wrote his own name, while Kemal’s is written by someone else, a novelist.

I began this essay with a suffix, and I want to end with one. The novel’s first sentence was built on -mış: anıymış, a past learned afterwards, indirectly, with surprise. The last is built on -dı: yaşadım, “I lived”. In Turkish this suffix says that the speaker knows the event first-hand, has witnessed it, and takes it on as his own knowledge.26 The novel thus travels from a mirative past to a witnessed one: the man who discovered his life with a stranger’s surprise ends by declaring it his own. In English the journey is invisible, since both sentences stand in the same simple past, “It was” and “I lived”; this is one of those places where a translation, however faithful, cannot show what the original does with a single syllable. It is hard to think of a grammatical movement that sums up the aim of psychoanalytic work better. In the Rome discourse Lacan says that at the basis of the method Freud called psychoanalysis lies the subject’s assumption of his own history, insofar as that history is constituted by speech addressed to the other.27 The Museum of Innocence takes both halves of the formula seriously. Kemal’s witnessed past does not come out of his own mouth but from another man’s pen; in order to be able to say “I lived”, he needed a witness, a writer, a museum. Perhaps happiness, at least in the sense this novel teaches, is something that is not content to be lived, and waits to be put into words before someone else.

Afterword

The nine-episode Netflix adaptation, directed by Zeynep Günay and written by Ertan Kurtulan, with Selahattin Paşalı as Kemal and Eylül Lize Kandemir as Füsun, was released on 13 February 2026 and has brought the novel a new generation of readers, many of them outside Turkey.28 The question it puts to its viewers is the exact reverse of the reading I have tried to set out above. In the novel we know Füsun and the objects only through Kemal’s words; the camera gives Füsun a face, a voice and a gaze of her own. How far can a woman constructed as the object of a collection overflow, on screen, the narrative of the man who collected her? It is a question anyone who watches the adaptation can ask, and perhaps should.
