Forbidden Love: Desire, the Law and the Mirror in Halit Ziya’s Aşk-ı Memnu

Haktan Tay · 19 October 2026 · 20 min read

The title of Halit Ziya’s novel puts forward a thesis before the first page is turned: Aşk-ı Memnu, forbidden love. This essay argues that the prohibition is not the obstacle to this love but its source, and that the novel can be read as the story of a repetition passed down among women from one generation to the next, in which a mother’s defeat looks at first like her daughter’s victory and then becomes her fate.

This essay first appeared in Turkish. The English version is my own; it adds some context for readers who know the novel only through its television adaptations, or not at all.

Concupiscentiam nesciebam, nisi lex diceret: Non concupisces.

Paul, Romans 7:7 (Vulgate)

I

In the seventh chapter of Paul’s letter to the Romans stands one of the most unsettling sentences Western thought has produced on the relation between desire and the law. In the King James Version: “I had not known lust, except the law had said, Thou shalt not covet.” For Paul the sentence is a confession. Sin is not born of the law, yet it is through the law that sin comes alive and makes itself known. In his seminar on ethics of 1959–1960, Lacan rereads the passage and puts in place of the word “sin” a term from his own vocabulary, the Thing: the absolute object of desire, never to be reached. The law does not merely repress desire; it brings its object into view and lends it a radiance that ordinary objects can never possess.1

Halit Ziya’s title compresses this thought into two words. An everyday reading hears in “forbidden love” a prohibition that has befallen love like a misfortune; the novel, as its pages advance, shows the relation running the other way. What makes Behlül precious to Bihter is less any quality of his own than the fact that he stands behind a prohibition. The novel is merciless on this point. Behlül, Adnan Bey’s nephew, is a light young man, a flirt, fond of being loved and incapable of any lasting bond. His whole attraction comes from where he stands: in the house of Bihter’s husband, as her husband’s kinsman, a figure she sees every day and can never possess. That the spell lifts when the prohibition lifts is something everyone who has lived a forbidden love knows; it is what the novel shows from Behlül’s side.

The word in the title carries the same structure. Memnu, from Arabic, derives from a root meaning to hinder, to withhold, to bar; what is memnu is what has had an obstacle set before it, what has been withheld. Turkish has a word that sounds almost the same, memnun, “pleased, content”, and its noun memnuniyet, “satisfaction”. They come from an entirely different root, but a Turkish ear cannot help hearing the prohibited and the pleasing side by side in the title, and this lends it an irony most readers never notice. In Halit Ziya’s novel, satisfaction seems possible only behind an obstacle, in the shadow of a prohibition.

II

Aşk-ı Memnu was serialised in 1899–1900 in the Istanbul review Servet-i Fünun, “The Wealth of Sciences”, and published as a book in 1900. It is the most accomplished novel of the group of writers gathered around that review, known as the Edebiyat-ı Cedide, the “New Literature”, and it is counted among the first great psychological novels in Turkish. It deserves the description. Halit Ziya is interested less in what his characters do in the outer world than in the fluctuations of their inner lives, and he gently opens to the reader what they hide from themselves. What makes this possible is free indirect style, which Halit Ziya was the first to handle with such mastery in Turkish prose. Sentences built by a third-person narrator slide, without our noticing, into the character’s inner voice; we hear Bihter’s thoughts not from her mouth but from her point of view, almost with her breathing. For a psychoanalytic reading the technique is a gift, for it makes visible in the texture of the sentence the gap between what a character says to herself and what she keeps from herself: the very place where the unconscious works.

Halit Ziya wrote the novel at thirty-three. Born in Istanbul in 1866 (some sources give 1865), he spent his childhood and youth in Izmir, where he learned French and read the French novel at first hand. In later years he became chief secretary to Sultan Mehmed V Reşad, and towards the end of his life he published his memoirs under the title Kırk Yıl, “Forty Years”.2 In old age he simplified the language of his novels himself, replacing many Arabic and Persian words with Turkish ones in new editions; the Aşk-ı Memnu most readers know today rests largely on this second, simplified text.3 That a novelist should rewrite his book thirty years on bears an odd resemblance to what the book itself tells: the past is rebuilt, afterwards, in another language. Critics have often compared the novel with Flaubert’s Madame Bovary, and the comparison is apt. What Jules de Gaultier named bovarysme in 1892, taking Flaubert’s heroine as his model, the power of a human being to conceive of herself as other than she is, also lies at the core of Bihter’s story.4

Another leg of the comparison is the relation of these novels to their societies. Flaubert was tried in 1857 for offences against public morals and religion over Madame Bovary, which had been serialised in the Revue de Paris the year before, and was acquitted. Tolstoy’s Anna Karenina, completed in 1877, opened with its famous sentence about happy families resembling one another and every unhappy family being unhappy in its own way; Anna too, like Bihter, would end a forbidden love by taking her own life.5 Aşk-ı Memnu faced no such trial. But Servet-i Fünun, the review in which it appeared, was closed a year later, in 1901, by the government of Abdülhamid II over an article translated from French, and the New Literature group broke up.6 In a period when censorship watched over everything, it is no accident that the whole drama of the novel takes place within the walls of a yalı, one of the wooden waterside mansions of the Bosphorus, in the silent relations of a family. The conflicts a society could not speak about had withdrawn into a house: into the looks of women, the space before mirrors, the words left unsaid.

But Bihter is no Emma Bovary. Emma’s dreams are fed by the novels she reads; the source of Bihter’s is much closer to home. It is in her mother’s house. Halit Ziya’s previous novel, Mai ve Siyah, “Blue and Black”, serialised in the same review in 1897, told of a young poet, Ahmet Cemil, crushed between the size of his dreams and the narrowness of his life; blue in the title was the colour of dreams, black the colour of reality.7 In Aşk-ı Memnu the opposition moves from a man’s literary dreams to a woman’s desire, from a writing desk to the rooms of a waterside house. The richest vein of the novel for psychoanalysis is that Bihter’s desire is, from the first moment, woven into the desire of another woman: her mother.

III

Bihter is the younger daughter of a family known along the Bosphorus as the “Melih Bey set”, after its late head. Her mother, Firdevs Hanım, is a widow, beautiful, unwilling to let go of her youth, and constantly talked about; Bihter’s elder sister, Peyker, is already married. The nickname is less the name of a family than the name of a piece of gossip: the point at which women’s desire has fallen into the mouth of the neighbourhood.

All the women of the novel live under the gaze of that language, that gossip. The waterside houses of the Bosphorus stand close together, rowing boats pass in front of one another, windows look into windows; who was seen with whom, who got into which boat, who entered whose house at what hour, travels along the shore from one mouth to the next. What Lacan calls the gaze functions here not as the eye of a particular person but as an eye that is everywhere and visible nowhere: the neighbourhood itself. Bihter’s whole life is built, before this gaze, on not becoming a woman who is “talked about” like her mother; and the novel is the story of her becoming exactly what that gaze most fears. Firdevs Hanım, who believes that the wealthy widower Adnan Bey has been paying her attention on their boating excursions, expects a proposal of marriage. The proposal comes; but it is for her daughter. The novel implies that Bihter knew of her mother’s hopes and watched her defeat with a quiet pleasure.

Bihter’s marriage thus achieves two things at once: an escape from her mother’s world and a victory over her mother. One of the oldest lessons of psychoanalysis is that what we flee is often what we repeat with the greatest fidelity. Bihter takes the man her mother wanted; she inherits her desire, so to speak, from her mother’s desire. In his paper “Female Sexuality” of 1931, Freud had shown that a girl’s attachment to her mother lasts much longer than had been supposed, that this bond is interwoven with a deep hostility, and that the daughter’s reproaches against the mother can persist into adult life.8 In 1972 Lacan names the relation to the mother, for many women, with a single harsh word: ravage, devastation.9 The silent war between Firdevs Hanım and Bihter is a version of that devastation set in a waterside house on the Bosphorus.

Firdevs Hanım is herself one of the novel’s most tragic figures, though Halit Ziya usually shows her in a mocking light. As her daughters grow up she grows older, and she refuses to accept it. Hers is a life spent among mirrors and dresses, trying to see herself, among the young, as a woman still desired. A type of woman Freud describes in his 1914 paper on narcissism, nourished less by loving than by seeing herself loved, is almost caricaturally distinct in Firdevs Hanım.10 Behind the caricature, though, lies a fear: that the gaze will withdraw from her, that she will no longer be seen, that she will cease to be the object of desire and turn into a mother, an old woman, a thing of the past. Bihter both scorns this fear in her mother and inherits it. What Bihter lives through before Nihal at the end of the novel is exactly what Firdevs Hanım lived through before Bihter: the feeling that, with the arrival of a younger woman, one’s own place has been taken.

IV

One of the novel’s most remembered scenes comes after Bihter returns from an outing to the Göksu, a stream on the Asian shore of the Bosphorus and a favourite promenade of Ottoman Istanbul. She undresses and contemplates her own body before the mirror. In the years when Halit Ziya wrote it, a Turkish novel in which a woman looked at her own body for so long and so directly was something extremely rare.

To read the scene merely as an awakening of sensuality is to make it smaller. Bihter before the mirror is looking at herself through someone else’s eyes, and the real tension of the scene comes from the uncertainty about who that someone is. Lacan’s concept of the mirror stage describes precisely this: the ego is first constituted outside, in an image, and as offered to a gaze. What Bihter discovers in the mirror is not the beauty of her body but the question of whom that beauty belongs to. For whom does this body exist? For her husband, for herself, for someone else not yet named?

In her famous paper of 1929, the English psychoanalyst Joan Riviere argued that womanliness cannot be told apart from a mask worn before the gaze of others.11 Lacan joins this idea to the question into which hysteria keeps throwing the subject. In his third seminar he says that at the heart of the hysterical question lies the question “What is a woman?”12 Bihter before the mirror asks this question caught between two answers that have been offered to her. One is her mother’s life: the woman who is talked about, whose desire has become the property of the neighbourhood. The other is the life her husband’s house offers: the respectable, sheltered wife, without desire. Bihter’s tragedy is that neither belongs to her; that both are places cut out for her by someone else, a mother or a husband.

V

Adnan Bey offers Bihter tenderness, wealth and a respectable position. But something is missing from the marriage, and the novel makes the lack felt for pages on end without ever naming it. In his 1912 paper “On the Universal Tendency to Debasement in the Sphere of Love”, Freud writes that in many people love is split into two currents, the tender and the sensual. Such people, in Freud’s unforgettable sentence, do not desire where they love, and cannot love where they desire.13 Aşk-ı Memnu distributes this split within a single house, between two men. Adnan Bey is on the side of tenderness, the law and order; Behlül on the side of desire. But Behlül himself is an emptiness. His value as an object of desire comes, like the object to which Paul’s law points, from the place where a prohibition has put him.

Behlül’s own desire bears the other face of the same split. The novel draws him as a young man who loves to be loved, who confirms himself in the attention of many women and can attach himself to none. He desires Bihter while she stands behind a prohibition; when the prohibition collapses, when Bihter truly belongs to him, his interest scatters. Like the man Freud describes, he cannot desire where he loves or love where he desires; his turn towards Nihal is perhaps an attempt to cross over, not to desire, but to the side of the respectable, safe woman, the one who is “to be loved”. Behlül’s emptiness is less a wickedness than the lightness of someone who has never once questioned his own desire.

VI

The moment he possesses Bihter, Behlül’s interest begins to scatter, and in the end he becomes engaged to Nihal, Adnan Bey’s daughter. Here perhaps the least noticed and sharpest structural feature of the novel comes into view. Firdevs Hanım lost the man she was expecting to a younger woman, her daughter Bihter. Now Bihter is losing the man she loves to a younger woman, her husband’s daughter Nihal. Bihter falls into the very middle of the place she fled: her mother’s position.

In Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920), Freud speaks of people whose lives seem to be ruled by a fate, and stresses that this fate is for the most part arranged by the subject’s own unconscious.14 Bihter’s is the novel of how women’s desire is handed down from one generation to the next, of how a mother’s defeat can look like her daughter’s victory and then turn into her fate. What Bihter feels towards Nihal is, from this angle, more than simple jealousy. Nihal is the being her father loves most; she holds, inside the house, a bond Bihter can never fully enter. Behlül’s turn towards Nihal mercilessly exposes how fragile Bihter’s place in this house is: she is neither its daughter nor its real mistress.

The least discussed figure in this triangle is perhaps Adnan Bey: a man who marries a woman of his daughter’s generation, and who does not see, or chooses not to see, what is happening in his own house until the very end. That Adnan Bey suspects nothing throughout the novel strikes the reader as almost unbelievable. The looks between his wife and his nephew, Bihter’s changing moods, the tension in the air of the house, all escape him. Psychoanalysis reads such blindness less as ignorance than as a wish not to know. Adnan Bey’s not-seeing is a way of not admitting that his own marriage is marked by a limit, by an age, by a lack, and of not facing a truth that could spoil his privileged bond with his daughter. When the truth comes out, the first thing Adnan Bey does is return to his daughter, to the old order, to their intimacy of two. The novel does not show his mourning; the house closes again over Bihter’s absence as though nothing had happened.

Nihal herself deserves as much attention as Bihter in a psychoanalytic reading. Halit Ziya draws her as a nervous, fragile girl, given to frequent attacks and sleepless nights. Nihal is passionately attached to her father; she lives his remarriage as a betrayal, and approaches her stepmother first coldly, then with hostility, then with a strange intimacy. The picture is strikingly reminiscent of Dora, the eighteen-year-old whom Freud was treating in Vienna in the same years. Dora too is attached to her father, and feels towards the woman with whom he is having an affair, Frau K., both jealousy and a deep admiration; her fits of coughing, her loss of voice, her thoughts of suicide are pieces of the picture Freud called hysteria.15 In Lacan’s reading of Dora, the girl’s interest in Frau K. expresses a curiosity about the secret of femininity: what is it that makes my father able to desire a woman? The same question can be read in Nihal’s gaze upon Bihter. Bihter is the woman her father loves; the woman Nihal is not yet, fears becoming, and perhaps wishes to become. At the end of the novel Nihal learns of Behlül’s betrayal and of Bihter’s secret at the same moment; and with this discovery she sees how dangerous a place it is to be desired as a woman. The return to the old intimacy of father and daughter is, from this angle, less a victory than a retreat: from the question of being a woman to the safe place of being her father’s daughter.

VII

Bihter’s mirror cannot fail to recall another famous mirror. In the tale of Snow White collected by the Brothers Grimm, the queen asks her mirror every day who is the fairest in the land; and one day the mirror tells her the answer is no longer the queen but the girl who is growing up. A little-known detail of the tale is almost unsettling from the point of view of Aşk-ı Memnu. In the first edition of 1812, the jealous queen is Snow White’s own mother; only in the edition of 1819 does she become a stepmother.16 In his book on the psychoanalytic reading of fairy tales, Bruno Bettelheim interprets Snow White as the staging of an Oedipal rivalry between mother and daughter: as the girl grows up she threatens the mother’s place, and the voice of the mirror is that threat finding words.17

Halit Ziya’s novel plays this structure twice, across two generations. Between Firdevs Hanım and Bihter there is the rivalry of a mother and her own daughter; between Bihter and Nihal, that of a stepmother and her stepdaughter. In the first scene Bihter is the girl, and wins; in the second she becomes the queen, and loses. The novel’s mirror, like the mirror of the tale, is a mirror that says time has passed. Nihal’s own place deserves separate thought here: a nervous girl who lost her mother early, passionately attached to her father, raised under the protection of her French governess, Mademoiselle de Courton. Freud had explicitly refused to call the girl’s attachment to her father an “Electra complex”, as Jung had proposed; for him the girl’s development was not a mirror image of the boy’s, but a separate and more circuitous path that begins with the attachment to the mother.18 At the end of the novel, when the house returns to the old intimacy of father and daughter, it is Nihal who wins; but the price of that victory remains a question the novel never asks aloud.

VIII

The end comes fast. Nihal witnesses a quarrel between Bihter and Behlül and learns of the secret affair; everything comes out. Behlül leaves the house. Bihter, having found nothing she hoped for, takes her own life. The novel closes with the house returning to its old order, and Adnan Bey and Nihal recovering their former intimacy.

Bihter’s final act is a literary picture of a moment psychoanalysis has thought about with care. In his seminar on anxiety of 1962–1963, Lacan distinguishes acting out from the passage à l’acte. In acting out, the subject remains on a stage; the behaviour is a message addressed to someone, waiting to be understood. In the passage to the act, the subject falls off the stage: under the gaze that rests on her, at a moment when no word and no message has any room left, she lets herself drop out of the world like an object.19 Lacan’s example is the young woman Freud wrote about in 1920: walking in the street with the woman she loves, she meets her father’s angry gaze, and in that instant throws herself over a wall onto a railway cutting. Bihter’s last moment too, as the novel tells it, follows upon a gaze, the gaze of Nihal and Adnan Bey, a moment in which all her secrets have been laid bare. To see this structure matters not in order to explain the fate of a literary heroine, but to remember how heavy such moments are in the clinic, and why, at the moment a subject sees herself under another’s gaze as nothing but a fallen object, a word, a listener, a limit can be vital. Around Bihter there is not one person she can talk to.

Read a hundred and twenty-five years later, this ending is disturbing, and it should be. The woman who follows her desire dies; the law and the order are restored; the novel, in keeping with the moral world of its time, seems to punish female desire. But that reading cannot account for the novel’s power. At the end of his seminar on ethics, Lacan reduces the ethics of psychoanalysis to a single question: Avez-vous agi en conformité avec le désir qui vous habite ? Have you acted in conformity with the desire that dwells in you?20 Bihter’s tragedy is that she can neither give up her desire nor bear it. Between her mother’s fate and her husband’s house, she finds no place where she could be the owner of her own desire. Her death is less the triumph of the law than the outcome of this impossible position; and the novel’s real question is why a third place, one where Bihter could have lived, never opened.

Afterword

The novel first reached the screen in 1975, in a six-part TRT series directed by Halit Refiğ, with Müjde Ar as Bihter. The seventy-nine-episode series broadcast on Kanal D between 2008 and 2010, starring Beren Saat, Kıvanç Tatlıtuğ, Selçuk Yöntem and Nebahat Çehre, became a social phenomenon; its finale entered the list of the most-watched broadcasts in the history of Turkish television.21 Sold abroad under the title Forbidden Love, the 2008 series is how most readers outside Turkey first meet Bihter; the novel itself has had to wait much longer. Penguin Classics has announced the first English translation, by Douglas Scott Brookes, under the title The House on the Bosphorus, for 2027.22

In the finale, millions of viewers pitied Bihter more than they judged her; many understood her. That a story set more than a century ago within the walls of a waterside house should feel so close to today’s audience is itself worth thinking about. The gossip of the neighbourhood has been replaced by the unending gaze of screens; who was seen with whom now travels not along the shore but from phone to phone. Yet a woman’s desire falling into other people’s mouths, a mother and a daughter standing in turn before the same mirror, a prohibition making desire shine, all seem unchanged. The reason, as I see it, is that forbidden love is a structural fact of desire before it is a moral matter: the prohibition makes the object shine, the obstacle feeds desire, repetition brings back what one fled. At this point psychoanalytic work offers neither a permission nor a prohibition; it tries to separate desire from the glitter of prohibitions. The question it asks is not “Is this forbidden?” but “What do I really desire, and whose desire is it?” This is perhaps the question Bihter could never ask. Through every page of the novel she seems to be looking for a place, a listener, a time in which to ask it; but in no room of the house, before no mirror, is there anyone to whom she could say it aloud.
